# Default stance and source hierarchy

Use this skill to write the editor-facing cover letter that travels with a manuscript: the
short argument that makes a Nature editor decide, in well under a minute, to send the paper
out for review instead of desk-rejecting it.

## Who reads it and why that shapes everything

The first reader is a handling editor, often a professional editor rather than a subfield
specialist, triaging many submissions a day. The letter is not graded for completeness; it
is scanned for three decisions:

- **Is this novel and important enough for this journal's bar?**
- **Is it in scope for this journal's audience?**
- **Is there any reason not to send it out (ethics, competing interest, dual submission)?**

Everything in the letter should serve one of those decisions. Anything that does not is
padding, and padding in a one-page letter is expensive.

## What a strong cover letter contains

- A **hook**: one or two sentences that make the advance feel consequential to a
  non-specialist, without jargon.
- The **gap and the advance**: what was unresolved, and what this paper now shows —
  the single most important result, stated plainly.
- **Calibrated significance**: why the advance matters, sized to the evidence, not inflated.
- An **explicit fit argument**: why *this* journal and its readership, specifically.
- **Declarations and logistics**: no competing/dual submission, any conflicts, suggested
  and excluded reviewers, and any author/ethics statements the journal requires.

## Default stance

- Treat the letter as persuasion to one busy editor, not a summary for readers. It is an
  argument, not a second abstract.
- Lead with the advance and its significance; do not open with throat-clearing about the
  field's general importance.
- Calibrate every significance claim to the evidence. An editor who senses hype discounts
  the whole letter. "Demonstrate" / "establish" / "suggest" must match what the data show.
- Make the journal-fit argument specific to the named journal. A paragraph that would read
  identically for any journal is a wasted paragraph and a desk-rejection signal.
- Never fabricate: no invented results, "first to…" claims, competing-paper comparisons,
  editor names, or reviewer identities. Missing facts become author-input flags.
- Respect the length budget: one page, roughly 250–500 words. Cut to fit; do not shrink the
  hook or the fit argument to make room for generic impact sentences.
- Keep this skill to the letter. Do not rewrite the abstract, polish the manuscript, or
  draft the paper's sections unless the user asks separately (route to nature-writing /
  nature-polishing).

## Source hierarchy

Use sources in this order:

1. Target journal submission guidelines and what the journal explicitly asks the cover
   letter to contain.
2. Nature Portfolio / Springer Nature author and submission guidance.
3. Established editor-facing cover-letter practice (hook, gap-advance, fit, declarations,
   length).
4. Field norms for the specific manuscript type.

If a submission-policy detail may have changed (required declarations, reviewer-suggestion
rules, presubmission-enquiry availability), verify the current journal page before final
advice.
