# Cover-letter anatomy and hook patterns

Open this when you need the paragraph-by-paragraph anatomy, concrete hook patterns, or the
length-budget detail.

## The editor's 60-second scan

The handling editor is deciding triage, not reading for pleasure. They look for: a clear
advance, a reason it matters broadly, and a reason it belongs here. Write so each of those
is findable in one pass. Front-load; an editor may not reach paragraph four.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph

### 1. Opening and hook

- Address the editor by name if known ("Dear Dr [name]"), else "Dear Editor".
- One line of housekeeping: manuscript title and that it is submitted for consideration as
  an [Article / Letter / etc.].
- Then the hook: one or two sentences stating why the advance is consequential, in language
  a non-specialist editor follows. The hook is the most important sentence in the letter.

Hook patterns that work:
- **Resolve a known open question:** "Whether X causes Y has remained unresolved because
  [obstacle]. We report direct evidence that…"
- **Break a standing limitation:** "[Field] has been limited by [constraint]; we show a
  [method/result] that removes it, enabling…"
- **Overturn an assumption:** "[Widely held belief] predicts Z; we find instead that…"
- **First quantitative/causal handle:** "We provide the first [to our knowledge, with
  basis] quantitative measurement of…"

Avoid openings that waste the hook slot: "We are pleased to submit our manuscript titled…"
as the first sentence, or a generic paragraph on how important the broad field is.

### 2. Gap → advance

- One or two sentences on the precise unresolved problem (not a literature review).
- Then the advance: "Here we show / we report…" with the single key result and its
  strongest quantitative support (the one number that matters, not all of them).
- Resist summarising every result; the abstract does that. The letter names the headline.

### 3. Significance

- What the result changes about understanding, capability, or practice.
- Who specifically should care (which communities), and why it is timely.
- Keep it concrete and bounded. "This reframes how the field models X" beats "This is a
  major breakthrough of broad importance."

### 4. Fit to journal

## Length budget detail

- One page, ~250–500 words, is the working target across Nature-family journals.
- Word counts by paragraph (rough): hook ~50–80, gap→advance ~70–110, significance ~50–90,
  fit ~40–70, declarations ~40–80.
- When over budget, cut in this order: generic impact sentences → background → secondary
  results → secondary significance. Never cut the hook or the fit argument to fit.
- Shorter is usually better. A tight 300-word letter that lands the three editor decisions
  beats a 500-word letter padded with impact slogans.
