# Fit-to-journal argument and significance calibration

Open this when you need to build the why-this-journal argument, match scope, or calibrate
significance against the evidence.

## Why the fit argument decides desk rejection

Nature-family journals reject most submissions without review, largely on scope and
perceived breadth of interest. The fit paragraph is where the author answers the editor's
real question: "Is this for my readers?" A generic fit paragraph — one that would read
identically for any journal — tells the editor the authors have not thought about fit, and
is itself a desk-rejection signal.

## Building a journal-specific fit argument

1. **Read the journal's stated scope and audience.** Each journal advertises what it wants
   (broad significance, methodological advance, disciplinary focus, clinical relevance).
   Name the journal's actual criterion, not a generic one.
2. **Match the advance to that criterion.** State, in one or two sentences, why this
   specific result meets this specific journal's bar — breadth of interest across fields,
   a methodological leap, timeliness, or relevance to the journal's core readership.
3. **Name the readership.** Which communities that read this journal will use or build on
   the result. Concrete communities beat "researchers in many fields".

Tiering, briefly:
- **Nature / Science / flagship multidisciplinary:** the case is broad significance and
  cross-disciplinary interest. The fit argument must show why non-specialists care.
- **Nature [subject] (e.g. Nature Methods, Nature Genetics):** the case is a top advance
  within that field's scope; show it is at the front of that specific field.
- **Nature Communications / broad-scope OA:** the case is solid, important advance of
  interest to a defined community; breadth bar is lower but significance still required.

If the target journal is unnamed, do not write a generic paragraph — flag the journal as
required input. The fit argument cannot be written without it.

## Significance calibration

Significance must be sized to the evidence. Editors and reviewers read overclaiming as a
credibility problem, and it is one of the fastest ways to lose a letter.

Calibrate the verb to the evidence, the same ladder nature-polishing uses:
- strong, direct, controlled evidence → "demonstrate", "establish", "show"
- consistent but indirect or single-system evidence → "indicate", "support"
- suggestive or preliminary evidence → "suggest", "raise the possibility"

Specific overclaim traps in cover letters:
- **"First / only / unprecedented"** — allowed only as "to our knowledge, the first…" with a
  basis the author can defend; otherwise cut. A bare "first" invites a reviewer to find a
  prior example.
- **Scope inflation** — claiming relevance to fields the work does not actually touch.
- **Impact slogans** — "of broad and lasting importance to science and society" carries no
  information and reads as padding.
- **Significance asserted, not shown** — "this is highly significant" without the one
  concrete thing it changes. Replace assertion with the specific change.

The test: every significance sentence should survive a skeptical editor asking "based on
what?" If it cannot, soften it or attach the evidence.
